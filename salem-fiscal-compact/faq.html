Questions

Fair questions, straight answers.

Doesn't this just mean cutting services?

No. Rules 1–3 are about how decisions get made, not what gets cut. Rule 4 is attrition, not layoffs. Rule 5 is revenue. The whole point is to stop the cycle where the library and parks absorb every shortfall.

Why not just pass a levy?

Voters have said no to the last asks, including the payroll tax in 2023. The Compact is what you do when the tax route is closed: stop the gap from growing, then earn from what the City already owns. If a future levy is ever going to pass, it'll be because residents trust that the rules are in place first.

What about homelessness?

The Compact doesn't cut homeless services. It conditions them on outcomes. If a program moves people into permanent housing and keeps them there, it should get more money. If it doesn't, it shouldn't. One public dashboard — exits to permanent housing, returns within six months, cost per bed — for every City dollar and every siting approval.

Can Council really do this without a vote of the people?

Rules 1, 3, and 4 are Council or City Manager action. Rule 2 starts as a resolution and should end as a charter amendment so a future Council can't quietly drop it. Rule 5's permanent fund needs a charter lock for the same reason. A state PILOT (payment in lieu of taxes) for the capitol campus is a Legislature fight and takes years — but it's the single largest structural handicap Salem carries.

Isn't "freeze hiring and use AI" just a slogan?

In police, fire, and direct services, yes — it doesn't apply and the Compact says so. In permitting, records, finance, customer service, and back-office work, it's a real and gradual lever: when a position turns over, the department has to show why tools and retrained staff can't absorb it before it's backfilled. The savings show up in years two through four, not year one, and bargaining agreements set the boundaries.

What does "City assets earn" actually mean?

The City of Salem is one of the largest landowners in town. Land should go into housing and mixed-use projects as equity or a 99-year participating ground lease where the City keeps a share of the upside — never a $1 transfer. Rooftops get solar and antenna leases. Parking gets modern pricing and EV charging. Facilities that go dark at 5 p.m. get rented. Proceeds seed a permanent fund whose principal is never spent, with a small capped draw to the General Fund every year, forever.

Will this close the deficit?

Not by itself, and we won't pretend otherwise. The General Fund is mostly police, fire, and retirement costs, and no rule changes that. The Compact stops the gap from growing while the City builds revenue from what it owns. That's the honest version.

Who's behind this?

Oregon Civic Voice, a Salem-based civic group. The Compact was drafted by residents with backgrounds in development, property management, and small business who've spent decades in the Willamette Valley. No party, no PAC, no candidate.
