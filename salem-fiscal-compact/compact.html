The one-pager

The Salem Fiscal Compact

Five rules to stop the bleeding. Print it, hand it to a councilor, put it in front of the Budget Committee.

Sign on

Salem's problem isn't one bad budget. It's a structure that lets the City promise more than it can carry, year after year, and then cut libraries and parks when the bill comes due. These five rules fix the structure. None of them require a new tax. Four of the five can be adopted by City Council resolution this year.

Rule 1 — One-time money pays for one-time things.

No ongoing program gets funded with reserves, grant windfalls, or land-sale proceeds. If a program will still exist in year three, it needs a year-three funding source before it's approved.

Approver: City Council (resolution). Cost: $0. Time: 60 days.

Rule 2 — Balance the five-year forecast, not just this year.

The City already publishes a five-year forecast. Adopt a rule that the forecast has to close — no adopted budget that relies on a deficit "to be solved later."

Approver: City Council (resolution now, charter amendment to make it permanent). Cost: $0.

Rule 3 — Name the money before you vote.

Every new program, position, or grant commitment comes to Council with a named, recurring funding source and a sunset date. No source, no vote.

Approver: City Council (procedural rule). Cost: $0. Time: 30 days.

Rule 4 — Grow through attrition, not layoffs.

Freeze net new hiring outside police and fire. When a back-office, permitting, records, or customer-service position turns over, the department has to show why AI-assisted tools and retrained staff can't absorb it before it's backfilled.

Approver: City Manager (administrative) with Council direction; subject to bargaining agreements. Savings: real but gradual — most of it lands in years 2–4.

Rule 5 — City assets earn. They don't get given away.

City land goes into projects as equity or a 99-year participating ground lease, never a $1 transfer. Rooftops, parking, right-of-way, fiber conduit, and naming rights get inventoried and put to work. The State pays something for the services the City provides to its tax-exempt capitol campus. Proceeds seed a permanent fund whose principal is never spent.

Approver: Council for the policy; Legislature for a state PILOT; voters for a permanent-fund charter lock. Time to first dollar: 12–36 months.

The accountability rider

Every City dollar that goes to homeless services, and every siting approval, comes with a condition: report outcomes on one public dashboard — exits to permanent housing, returns within six months, cost per bed. Regional reports show shelter capacity has grown sharply since 2020 while the annual count keeps rising. More beds without better outcomes is not a plan. Pay for results.

What this doesn't do

It doesn't close the gap by itself. The General Fund is mostly police, fire, and retirement costs, and no rule changes that. What it does is stop the gap from growing while the City builds real revenue from the assets it already owns — which is the only path voters haven't already rejected.
