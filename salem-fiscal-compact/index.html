Salem doesn't have a revenue problem. It has a rules problem.

Five rules the City Council can adopt this year — without a new tax — that stop the structural deficit from growing. Four of the five cost nothing.

The problem

The same loop, every budget season

The City forecasts a shortfall. Council debates which service to cut. The library, the parks, Center 50+, and eventually police and fire staffing end up on the table. Voters get asked for a new tax, and they say no — as they did with the payroll tax in 2023.

Shortfall→ Cut services→ Ask for a tax→ Voters say no→ Repeat

The reason it keeps happening isn't that the City Manager is bad at math. It's that nothing in Salem's rules stops Council from approving spending the five-year forecast can't carry. Good intentions get voted in; the bill arrives three years later.

The fix

Five rules that change the structure

Rules are what Council actually controls. These are written the way any business would write them.

1

One-time money pays for one-time things.

No ongoing program gets funded with reserves, grant windfalls, or land-sale proceeds. If a program will still exist in year three, it needs a year-three funding source before it's approved.

Approver: City Council (resolution)Cost: $0Time: 60 days
2

Balance the five-year forecast, not just this year.

The City already publishes a five-year forecast. Adopt a rule that the forecast has to close — no adopted budget that relies on a deficit "to be solved later."

Approver: Council resolution now; charter amendment to make it permanentCost: $0
3

Name the money before you vote.

Every new program, position, or grant commitment comes to Council with a named, recurring funding source and a sunset date. No source, no vote.

Approver: City Council (procedural rule)Cost: $0Time: 30 days
4

Grow through attrition, not layoffs.

Freeze net new hiring outside police and fire. When a back-office, permitting, records, or customer-service position turns over, the department has to show why AI-assisted tools and retrained staff can't absorb it before it's backfilled.

Approver: City Manager with Council direction; subject to bargaining agreementsSavings: real but gradual — most lands in years 2–4
5

City assets earn. They don't get given away.

City land goes into projects as equity or a 99-year participating ground lease, never a $1 transfer. Rooftops, parking, right-of-way, fiber conduit, and naming rights get inventoried and put to work. The State pays something for the services Salem provides to its tax-exempt capitol campus. Proceeds seed a permanent fund whose principal is never spent.

Approver: Council for policy; Legislature for a state PILOT; voters for a permanent-fund charter lockTime to first dollar: 12–36 months
+

The accountability rider.

Every City dollar that goes to homeless services, and every siting approval, comes with a condition: report outcomes on one public dashboard — exits to permanent housing, returns within six months, cost per bed. More beds without better outcomes is not a plan. Pay for results.

Approver: City Manager (contract terms) and Council (siting conditions)

What this doesn't do

It doesn't close the gap by itself. The General Fund is mostly police, fire, and retirement costs, and no rule changes that. What it does is stop the gap from growing while the City builds real revenue from the assets it already owns — which is the only path voters haven't already rejected.
